The Journey · That Was Then, This Is Now

Know You’re Gonna Get to the Top

Sep 25 – Oct 7, 2024 · Eastern Oregon → Eugene · Posted from the road on Day 1,298 · 66,769.6 documented miles — this tour

ALL DOWNHILL TO THE OCEAN — Film #6 Chapter Five, the film, 2:21 · also on the Ride Or Die America Page

September 25, 2024. He left the Oregon Trail Memorial and vanished into a week nobody saw — no posts, no cars, no traffic. A rail trail running straight through the pines. The Snake River crossed somewhere around Riggins. A good little rest stop at the Baker City memorial. A hot-and-cold canyon on the Journey Through Time byway, and a bunk in a hostel he’d stayed in once before. End of September up in that country is not all fun and games — it gets cold, fast — and the only way through a silent week like that is to grind.

You know the mountain in front of you. You’ve been circling it — waiting to feel ready, waiting for proof you’ll make it before you take the first step. That proof doesn’t exist at the bottom. It never has. The people who get to the top aren’t the ones who felt sure. They’re the ones who decided.

In Prineville, Marcel’s three kids — grown in four years — played him another round of symphony on the porch, and nothing else had changed: same sauna room, same home-cooked meal, the mattress exchange remembered. At dawn on October 2 he posted “McKenzie Pass, I’m coming for ya” and climbed the last pass of the whole crossing — over 237 pounds of bike, trailer, and gear hauled up through the burned snags to the summit sign. Up there, over the lava fields, he said the entire journey out loud: “I made it from the Atlantic through the Pacific to Canada to Mexico and back and up and down and all around.” Down the other side it was a quick roll: a stranger’s garage on October 3 — hamburgers and a ball game — then Eugene on October 4, where Jennifer Sullivan took him in for a couple of days and resupplied him with a Walmart run: a bike angel for the third time in four years. The Pacific: reached. Phase One: closed.

Ask him about that climb and he’ll give you the strangest receipt of the whole chapter: he can barely remember it. “The climb didn’t really matter, man. It more than likely was probably brutal. I don’t really know.” What he remembers is the decision underneath it: “I knew I was going to get to the top and that’s all that mattered.” And what the top felt like: “All the crying was over for a while because it’s all and I do mean all downhill to the ocean.” That’s the doctrine. Certainty isn’t a feeling you wait for at the bottom. It’s a sentence you say before the mountain — and then you climb like it’s already true.

His words for the summit itself: “The summits are where it’s always at. There’s no explaining it to anybody. Until they’ve done it.” Nobody can hand you that feeling — but the decision that earns it transfers to anybody, on any mountain. The knowing comes first. The climb doesn’t get a vote.

The chapter carries its own original duet, written for this film: “ALL DOWNHILL TO THE OCEAN.” The man’s voice carries the fall, the doubt, the get-back-up commands. The woman’s voice carries the dawn, the worthiness, the belief. The chorus is both of them together, and it is the line this chapter leaves in your hands:

“Know you’re gonna get to the top.”

The lesson — THE DISCIPLINE: Name your mountain — the one thing you’ve been circling. Say it out loud: “I know I’m gonna get to the top.” Then take the first step today, however small: one call, one page, one mile, one rep. Say it first. Step second. It takes under five minutes, tonight or tomorrow — and it compounds.
