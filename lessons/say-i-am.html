Ride It Till It Breaks

July 14 – July 31, 2024 · Wyoming → Dubois, Wyoming · Posted from the road on Day 1,298 · 66,769.6 documented miles — this tour

RIDE IT TILL IT BREAKS — Film #6 Chapter Three, the film, 3:24 · also on the Ride Or Die America Page · YouTube · YouTube Short · Instagram · TikTok · Coach’s profile

July 14, 2024. He crossed into Wyoming and didn’t post a word about it for three days. A pastor’s house in Bairoil. A cyclist hostel at the church in Jeffrey City. Then Lander, where his sleeping pad was shot and he had no money for a new one — so the outdoor shop’s manager, who had tried every trick he knew to fix the old one, walked into the city park with his wife and their dog and handed him a new one: “I got you another one. And it’s on us.”

You know this stretch of your own life. It’s the moment the thing you’ve been riding — the job, the plan, the version of you that’s been making noise since way back — finally breaks in a place with no shop, no backup, and no audience. No applause out there. No cheering section. Just heat, a broken machine, and a question: who do you say you are when nothing around you agrees?

His bottom bracket had been dying since Florida — seized in every shop from there to Wyoming. When the Lander shop said three, four, five days for a new bike, he said no: “I’ll ride it till it breaks.” On July 28 — his birthday — on Wind River Reservation, it broke. The bracket came out completely, 156 miles from the next bike shop, on the hottest day of the stretch. So he worked the phones from the roadside and sold a dealer out of Jackson Hole on driving a new bike to him on his birthday — and the dealer did, to Mike Tornesello’s house in Dubois. He gave the dealer the old bike, all 21,000 miles of it, for the delivery charge.

Then he took the new bike up its first pass unassisted — fourteen miles of climbing with no power, every muscle he owned on fire — because he had to learn what he could do on it before he let it carry him. And out there the whole thing turned: the ego contest he’d been dreaming up — outride me if you can, last man riding — died on that reservation road. His words: “I don’t need applause. I don’t need a cheering section. I don’t need sponsors. There’s gotta be purpose in it all.”

The chapter carries its own original duet, written for this film: “SAY I AM.” The man’s voice carries the fall, the doubt, the get-back-up commands. The woman’s voice carries the dawn, the worthiness, the belief. The chorus is both of them together, and it is the line this chapter leaves in your hands:

“Say I am — then be it.”

The lesson — THE DISCIPLINE: The name comes first. It’s a mind trick he plays: if he calls it the beast, it’s gotta perform like the beast; if he wears a shirt that says beast, he’s gotta perform like a beast. The bike was never the beast — the name lands on his tools because they’re his. He is. And the name you wear is the work you owe. So tonight, say your “I am” out loud — one strong, true sentence about who you are: I’m strong. I’m powerful. I’m still here. Tomorrow, do one thing that matches the name.

R.O.D.A. — Ride Out Depleted Anyway.
